02 — What survives the week

Most of a standard card is noise by Sunday

I treated every number on the card as a forecast for the next series and scored it the way forecasters score a forecast: how far it landed from what happened. A six-game sample is noisy by itself, so the score removes the coin-flip part of the in-series result and keeps only the distance from the hitter's true rate that week. The yardstick is the error of assuming a league-average hitter, which is what you know with no report at all.

The test is every Triple-A hitter in every 2026 series: {{n_hitter_series}} hitter-series across {{n_series}} series. Every setting (how fast old games fade, how much last season counts, how hard each number shrinks) was tuned on 2025 only.

Printed raw, {{n_worse}} of {{n_card}} numbers did worse than league average ({{n_worse_sure}} by more than the uncertainty). Only the swing-decision numbers beat it as printed: swing rate, chase rate, zone swing rate, first-pitch swing rate and whiff rate. Every last-14-days line did worse.

The Six-Day Forecast beat league average on all {{n_card}}, and beat the season line on all {{n_card}}, by a median of {{cut_vs_season}}%. The same held one level down, in Jupiter's Florida State League: {{fsl_n_worse}} of {{fsl_n_card}} season lines worse than league average, and the forecast better on all {{fsl_n_card}}.

Why: every number has a crossover point

A raw rate built on n chances has about M/n times the error of the league average, where M is that number's stabilization point: the sample at which a hitter's own data and the league average deserve equal weight. Below M chances, the raw number is worse than knowing nothing. Shrinking by M/(n+M) is never worse than either, which is all the Six-Day Forecast does, number by number.

In Triple-A, chase rate crosses over at about {{M_chase}} pitches out of the zone and first-pitch swing rate at about {{M_fps}} first pitches. Strikeout rate takes about {{M_k}} plate appearances, batting average {{M_avg}} at-bats, and BABIP {{M_babip}} balls in play, more than most hitters get in a season.

03 — Does the book go stale by Sunday?

Hitters do not catch up over a series

Minor league teams play the same opponent six days in a row, so relievers often face the same hitters twice in a week. If hitters learn a pitcher on Tuesday, Monday's plan should change by Saturday. I tested it on {{fam_pas}} Triple-A plate appearances from 2024 to 2026, {{fam_familiar}} of them against a pitcher the hitter had already faced earlier in that series.

The comparison is inside the same pitcher outing: hitters who already saw him that week against hitters seeing him for the first time, with each hitter's own level, platoon side and times through the order held fixed. That cancels out his stuff that day, his fatigue, the park and the umpire.

Expected wOBA moved {{fam_xw}} points (95% interval {{fam_lo}} to {{fam_hi}}). Strikeouts dipped by {{fam_k}} points, a small edge in putting the ball in play that did not turn into damage. For scale, the same design finds the known effects plainly: the third time through the order adds {{tto3}} points and the platoon edge adds {{platoon}}. Jupiter's league shows the same: {{fsl_fam_xw}} points ({{fsl_fam_lo}} to {{fsl_fam_hi}}).

For a Fellow: plan the week from Monday's packet. The adjustment that pays is inside a game, the third time through, not across the series.

04 — Is it working?

Pitch shape answers in days. Plate discipline answers in months.

A development plan needs check-in dates, and the right date depends on what is being changed. The same noise math as the card says how long a change has to run before it is clear of chance, at a regular's usual workload. A one-mile-per-hour velocity gain is clear after {{velo_phrase}}; a three-degree arm-angle change after {{arm_phrase}}. A five-point drop in chase rate takes about {{chase_weeks}} weeks of a regular's pitches, and a five-point jump in hard-hit rate takes about {{hh_weeks}} weeks, close to a whole minor league season.

05 — How it is built

Method, data and limits

Data

Pitch-level Hawk-Eye data from Baseball Savant's public minor league search: every Triple-A game and every Florida State League game from 2024 to 2026 ({{n_pitches}} pitches), plus schedules, rosters on each Monday, and player details from the MLB Stats API. Those are the two levels with public tracking, which is why Pensacola (Double-A) and Beloit (High-A) are not here. Daytona's park has no pitch tracking, so those games count for results but not for pitch metrics.

The forecast

Each number is a beta-binomial posterior: (k + M·μ) / (n + M), where k of n is the hitter's record (recent games weighted more, last season at a discount) and μ is the league rate that day. Splits and zone halves shrink toward the hitter's own overall forecast instead of the league. The half-life, last-season weight and M for every number were picked on 2025 series and frozen before 2026 was scored. "Trust" on the card is n/(n+M): the share of the number that is his own data.

Scoring

For each hitter-series, the squared distance from the forecast to the in-series rate, minus the in-series binomial noise (an unbiased correction), weighted by chances. The ratio to the league-average forecast's error is what the charts show, with intervals from resampling whole series. Numbers with no measurable spread between hitters inside a series are marked rather than scored.

Checks

The estimators are tested on simulated data with known answers: the crossover math, the noise correction, recovering a planted stabilization point, the change tests' false-alarm rates, and the fixed-effects design recovering a planted effect. A test also confirms that a forecast never sees the day it is forecasting. Every number on this page is filled in from the pipeline's output, not typed. make all rebuilds everything from the raw downloads.

Limits

Public data only: no bat tracking, no biomechanics, no internal grades or development plans, which a club's packet would add. The development receipts are changes the data shows, not the staff's actual plans. Pitch types are Savant's labels, which is why the tracker checks for relabels. The Spanish uses the terms MLB's Spanish-language coverage uses (swings en blanco, macetazos, batazos duros), and a bilingual coach should still read every template before it goes on a clubhouse wall.

The flags

The header spells S–D–F in the International Code of Signals, the flag alphabet ships still use, and each section is marked with its numeral pennant. Every affiliate in the Marlins system is named for something in the water, so the packet borrows from the people who forecast it.
